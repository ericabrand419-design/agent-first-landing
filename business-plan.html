1 · Business Plan

Executive Summary

The company, opportunity, and current plan in one place

Agent First is an operational decision-support platform for rules-heavy frontline work. It converts organization-approved policies, scripts, exceptions, contacts, and workflow rules into context-specific guidance that appears while a human worker is serving a customer or making an operational decision.

The company thesis is that knowledge is not operational simply because it exists. Organizations can have extensive documentation and still force a worker to search, interpret, remember which client a rule belongs to, translate policy into customer-facing language, and decide the next action while a live interaction continues.

Company thesisMake approved organizational knowledge executable in the moment without turning the frontline worker into a human search engine and without silently transferring policy authority to an AI model.

The first commercial wedge is multi-client contact-center and outsourced service work. One worker may move among multiple client, account, location, or program rule sets during a shift, making wrong-context errors, search time, supervisor escalation, and training burden visible and measurable.

Agent First is early commercial-stage. A working private demo exists across five fictional industries and ten fictional organizations with profile switching, role-aware Agent/Supervisor/Admin experiences, scripts, source detail, memory, quick actions, Test Mode, Guided Demo, and an Admin implementation workflow. The pilot operating package, pricing framework, security/data overview, Deal Room, and automated presentation regression are also built. These are product and operating-system milestones; they are not proof of customer ROI, retention, willingness to pay, or product-market fit.

The commercial model is pilot-to-subscription B2B software. Accepted founding pilot partners use Agent First at the standard $69 per agent per month Starter rate during a live pilot of up to 90 days, with a 10-seat minimum ($690 per month). The standard $2,500 Client Profile implementation fee is waived for the primary pilot Profile, no Growth-tier volume discount applies during the pilot, and a one-time $500 credit applies to the first post-pilot invoice if the pilot converts. One bounded workflow family and one primary Client Profile are included by default; additional Profiles, major integrations, or expanded scope are separately scoped. Published standard pricing remains Starter at $69 per agent per month, qualifying Growth at $49 per agent per month, Enterprise custom, and a $2,500 one-time Client Profile implementation fee for standard deployments.

The company does not need to prove that buyers care about AI for service operations. It must prove a narrower advantage in a crowded category: faster implementation of approved rules, clearer context, lower frontline cognitive burden, better governance, measurable decision quality, and a lightweight deployment path that does not require replacing the customer’s CCaaS, CRM, ticketing, workforce, or scheduling stack.

2 · Business Plan

Company Thesis and Category

Agent-first design as product strategy and operating philosophy

Agent First begins with a design principle: software should absorb more organizational complexity so the worker can spend more attention on the customer, the exception, and the human judgment that remains necessary. This is not an argument against automation. It is a sequencing argument: make operating rules legible, versioned, attributable, testable, and usable before delegating consequential work more broadly.

Category

Agent First sits at the intersection of agent assist, knowledge management, workflow guidance, AI copilots, and frontline enablement. The proposed category distinction is operational decision support: software organized around the decision a worker must make in a specific client or operating context, rather than around the document the worker must find.

Agent First isContext-specific decision, customer wording, next action, source, and operating context.
Agent First is notA replacement CCaaS/CRM/ticketing system or a generic enterprise search box.
AI boundaryModels assist with interpretation, retrieval, structure, or wording; the organization remains policy authority.
3 · Business Plan

The Problem

The operational gap between stored knowledge and correct action

Frontline teams commonly work inside information architectures designed for storage and administration rather than live decision-making: intranets, PDFs, knowledge bases, shared drives, CRM notes, scripts, spreadsheets, supervisor messages, and tribal knowledge.

Five forms of knowledge friction

  1. Context ambiguity: the correct rule changes by client, location, product, program, customer type, advisor, time, exception, or service condition.
  2. Search friction: the worker must locate the right source while maintaining the interaction.
  3. Interpretation friction: policy may not express the exact next action, wording, documentation, escalation, or exception path.
  4. Memory burden: experienced workers carry shortcuts and exceptions newer workers do not know.
  5. Change friction: updated policy competes with old behavior stored in memory, screenshots, notes, and secondary systems.

The expected operational consequences are longer resolution time, more supervisor pull-ins, slower ramp, inconsistent answers, wrong-context rule application, customer friction, and governance risk when unofficial workarounds become operationally influential.

5 · Business Plan

Target Customer and Ideal Customer Profile

Who should buy first — and who should not

The first customer should have a bounded workflow with repeated decisions, meaningful context variance, approved source material, a measurable baseline, and a named owner who can validate policy meaning.

Working early ICP

  • Multi-client contact centers, outsourced service providers, shared-service teams, centralized scheduling/intake, and other frontline environments that switch among multiple client or program rule sets.
  • Approximately 25–500 frontline users is a working early-market targeting range, not a validated segment boundary.
  • Frequent repeated decisions with client/account/location/service/exception-specific rules.
  • Existing approved policies, scripts, procedures, contacts, routing, and escalation paths.
  • A meaningful cost to search, escalation, transfer, training, inconsistency, or wrong-context handling.

Poor early fit

  • Very small teams with little rule variance and low search/escalation cost.
  • Primarily unstructured expert judgment with no authoritative source material.
  • Buyers seeking autonomous customer-facing AI with no human-assist workflow.
  • Deployments requiring deep integration, regulated production data, or enterprise certifications before core value can be tested.

Differentiation and Defensibility

Current differentiation hypotheses

  1. Profile-first operating model: active client/location/program is explicit rather than inferred from generic search.
  2. Decision-first interface: operational outcome, wording, next step, and source are the primary objects.
  3. Rule authority and governance: customer policy ownership, source controls, versioning, and review are first-class.
  4. Lightweight entry: start with a bounded configuration rather than replacing the customer stack.
  5. Built-in measurement and training: Test Mode, scenario pools, baseline metrics, and pilot scorecards are part of the system.
  6. Cross-industry schema: designed around recurring rule complexity rather than one vertical only.

What is not a moat

  • Using an LLM.
  • Real-time suggestions.
  • RAG or source citation alone.
  • A polished demo.
  • Founder intuition without product data, workflow IP, customer trust, and repeatable deployment know-how.

Potential defensibility accumulates through the structured Client Profile schema, validation workflow, rule patterns, evaluation scenarios, deployment data showing which guidance improves performance, product integrations, customer-specific governance history, and repeatable implementation economics.

13 · Business Plan

Pilot Strategy

Prove one workflow before selling transformation

The pilot is the bridge between product demonstration and commercial evidence. It is not a free trial and should not become unpaid custom development. The customer pays the standard Starter seat rate during a bounded live pilot of up to 90 days, with a 10-seat minimum, while Agent First absorbs the standard Client Profile implementation fee for the accepted primary pilot Profile.

Qualification gates

  • Rules-heavy workflow with repeated decisions and context variance.
  • Approved rules, scripts, contacts, exceptions, and validators exist.
  • Current workflow can be observed or tested to create a baseline.
  • Named decision owner plus supervisors/frontline users.
  • One workflow can be bounded without major integration.
  • Organization is willing to pay, participate, and make a continuation decision.

Pilot sequence

  1. Discovery and workflow selection.
  2. Source intake and workflow mapping.
  3. Client Profile configuration.
  4. Rule-owner validation and gap resolution.
  5. Baseline and controlled pre-launch testing.
  6. User readiness and launch gate.
  7. Bounded live use with weekly review.
  8. Post-pilot controlled test and evidence analysis.
  9. Final readout: continue, expand, revise, or stop.

Default measures include decision resolution time, correct-client rule application, first-contact accuracy, supervisor pull-ins, and controlled scenario/ramp performance. Targets are hypotheses agreed at kickoff, not guaranteed outcomes.

16 · Business Plan

Operating Model

High-touch learning now; repeatable delivery later

The early operating model is intentionally hands-on. Each implementation should teach the company how to structure sources, resolve ambiguity, validate rules, configure the interface, and measure the workflow. Repeated manual work should be instrumented and either intentionally retained because it creates value or placed into the productization backlog.

Core loop

  1. Qualify workflow and sponsor.
  2. Collect authorized source material.
  3. Structure material into a Client Profile and rule objects.
  4. Flag conflicts, missing sources, and ambiguity rather than guessing.
  5. Validate with customer rule owner.
  6. Test scenarios.
  7. Publish/version approved configuration.
  8. Monitor issues and rule changes.
  9. Measure workflow and produce readout.
  10. Feed repeated implementation patterns into tooling.

Product/founder discovery, sales, and implementation remain founder-involved through the first pilots. Engineering, security/architecture, and customer-success capacity should be added when actual bottlenecks appear, not because a generic startup org chart says they should.

Data, Security, and AI Governance

Security is a gate between prototype evaluation and production pilot use

The current private evaluator uses invited account activation, email/password access, password hashing, time-limited sessions, role-aware views, HTTPS delivery, and server-side hashed session tokens. The product is evaluation-ready for controlled demos and fictional/de-identified workflow testing; production-pilot readiness is a later state that requires Security Readiness Gate sign-off for the scoped proprietary-data environment. The company should continue saying exactly what exists today and exactly what has not been independently verified or certified.

Security readiness gateAgent First can continue product demonstrations, controlled testing, and pilot preparation with fictional or appropriately de-identified material. The company should not accept a customer's proprietary production procedures, client-specific operating data, or sensitive production information into a live production pilot until an experienced security-minded SaaS engineer or application-security consultant has reviewed the actual architecture, critical findings have been remediated, and the hardened pilot environment has passed the readiness gate.

Required pre-production security engagement

  • Architecture and threat model: document systems, trust boundaries, data flows, assets, attack surfaces, and likely failure modes.
  • Authentication and sessions: review activation, password handling, reset/recovery path, session lifecycle, MFA strategy, and eventual SSO.
  • Authorization and tenant isolation: verify server-side enforcement for Agent, Supervisor, Admin, Agent First staff, company/tenant, and Client Profile boundaries; test that URL/API/storage manipulation cannot cross those boundaries.
  • Database and API security: test object ownership, query scoping, API authorization, input validation, and administrative mutation paths.
  • Secrets and infrastructure: inventory credentials, environment variables, API keys, deployment/GitHub permissions, and service tokens.
  • Encryption and storage: verify TLS in transit and actual encryption-at-rest behavior across each datastore/provider used in the pilot architecture.
  • AI/model-provider data flow: document exactly what customer material is sent to each provider, retention/training settings, contractual controls, and how cross-customer context leakage is prevented.
  • Uploads and source ingestion: review file validation, storage, malware/content handling, authorization, and deletion behavior.
  • Logging and audit: capture authentication/access-control/admin events without unnecessarily placing sensitive data in logs; define alerting and review.
  • Backups, recovery, retention, and deletion: document restore expectations, customer deletion/export paths, and what survives in backups/logs.
  • Dependency and supply-chain security: review packages, build/deploy chain, repository access, automated dependency/vulnerability scanning, and update practices.
  • Security testing and incident response: code review/scanning, targeted penetration testing, vulnerability handling, incident contacts, decision rights, and customer notification process.
  • Verification framework: map the application against OWASP ASVS 5.0 and use NIST SSDF as a secure-development operating framework; these are verification/development references, not certifications.

Pilot data boundary

  • Before the security gate: use the minimum information necessary, favor approved rules that are not sensitive, and use fictional/de-identified scenarios for product and workflow evaluation.
  • After the security gate: admit only the specific proprietary production data categories explicitly authorized for the scoped pilot and supported by the reviewed architecture.
  • Exclude regulated or unusually sensitive data unless the deployment, contracts, controls, and buyer requirements have been specifically reviewed.
  • Collect operational metrics only when tied to agreed hypotheses.
  • Use recordings/transcripts only with authorization and contractual permission.

Governance authority

The customer rule owner defines policy meaning, exceptions, wording, routing, effective version, and approval. Agent First implementation structures and tests the material. AI can assist with classification/retrieval/drafting but does not become policy authority. The frontline user retains responsibility for the human action within organizational policy.

Compliance sequence

Prototype/demo → security architecture review → remediate critical findings → hardened pilot environment → real production pilots → formal compliance program when customers or regulated use cases actually require it.

  • Formal architecture/data-flow documentation and threat model come before production pilot data.
  • Granular RBAC, tenant isolation, audit history, retention/deletion, recovery, and incident procedures must be tested rather than assumed.
  • Penetration testing and vulnerability-management depth should increase with production exposure and buyer requirements.
  • SOC 2 readiness/attestation is not a day-one requirement; pursue it when the target segment or procurement process makes the assurance economically necessary.
  • HIPAA/BAA, PCI, or other regimes are addressed only when genuinely applicable to the architecture and customer scope.

  3. Test whether seat revenue during the up-to-90-day pilot plus the waived implementation investment produces acceptable pilot economics and a credible path to recurring margin.
  4. Use first conversions to test $69/$49 seat pricing, the $2,500 Client Profile fee for standard deployments, and whether the $500 conversion credit improves continuation without weakening price integrity.
  5. Avoid economically unbounded custom integrations unless separately paid.
  6. Delay permanent headcount until repeatable bottlenecks appear.

The separate Financial Model should contain conservative, base, and upside scenarios with editable assumptions. Required inputs include pipeline-to-pilot conversion, pilot cost, pilot-to-subscription conversion, initial seats/profiles, expansion, retention/churn, configuration hours, model/hosting cost, sales cycle, and CAC.

Organization and Founder

A founder-led company transitioning from insight to repeatable system

Agent First is founded by Erica Love. The company has been developed from direct observation of frontline knowledge friction and deliberately expanded into a multi-industry product thesis rather than remaining tied to one operational environment.

The founder advantage is closeness to the workflow problem. That advantage becomes durable only if it is translated into documented product principles, customer evidence, implementation playbooks, technical systems, and a team that can operate without the founder making every decision.

  • Keep founder ownership of customer discovery, product thesis, and first design-partner selection.
  • Add strong engineering capacity before live commitments exceed the current product stack’s reliability.
  • Use specialist security/legal/architecture support before enterprise contractual commitments.
  • Document implementation and rule-governance workflows.
  • Build an advisory network around contact-center operations, enterprise SaaS, security, B2B sales, and responsible AI.
